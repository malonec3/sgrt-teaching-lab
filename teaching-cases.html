How the simulation works

The surfaces. Each surface is a height field z = h(x, y). The reference is sampled on a regular grid with surface normals from central differences. The live capture is sampled independently at jittered positions from the same height field, so the two point sets never share vertices — as in a real second capture.

The registration. Point-to-plane ICP solving for translation only, with nearest-neighbour correspondences, rejection of matches on the ROI boundary, and trimming of the worst 10% of matches. It stops when the step falls below 10 nm, or when the last six iterates sit within 0.25 mm of each other (with noise, nearest-neighbour ICP commonly settles into a small cycle; the cycle average is used). A run that walks out of the capture range or loses its overlap is flagged and applies no correction.

Why flat fails. The normal equations are A·t = b with A = Σ nnᵀ over the matched points. For a plane every normal is identical, so A has rank 1: only the direction along the normal is solvable. The two in-plane directions have zero eigenvalue and the solver returns nothing for them. A tilted plate is no better — it just spreads the one solvable direction across all three axes, which shows up as cross-talk between them.

When the solve runs away. A direction that is only just above the conditioning cut-off can be less stable than one clearly below it: the solver trusts it and divides by an almost-zero eigenvalue, so any perturbation is amplified into a large reported shift. Fractions where the accumulated translation leaves any plausible capture range are stopped and marked as a registration failure in the log. If you see those, look at the condition number — you are sitting on the cliff edge between solvable and blind.

Degenerate directions. The solver uses a truncated pseudo-inverse: any eigen-direction whose eigenvalue falls below the conditioning cut-off relative to the largest is left uncorrected rather than being amplified into noise. This is a choice in this teaching model; vendor algorithms can behave differently.

Predicted uncertainty. With camera noise σn along the viewing axis, the estimator covariance is σn²·A⁺AzA⁺ where Az = Σ nz²nnᵀ. The predicted 1σ figures in the panel above are an approximate, local noise-only estimate from that expression, using an assumed effective point count of 75% of the requested samples. They exclude deformation, correspondence error and lost directions.

Two kinds of averaging. Independent, zero-mean noise decreases approximately as 1/√N when estimating a shared shift. A local anatomical change can also be diluted by unchanged surface within a larger ROI. These are different effects. Correlated change may produce a persistent bias; a small ROI may also lose useful landmarks.

What is left out. Rotations, real camera geometry and occlusion, and everything clinical: couch mechanics, immobilisation, intrafraction drift. Only height geometry is used, without colour-texture tracking or an internal target. Treat the qualitative behaviour, not the absolute magnitude, as the lesson.
